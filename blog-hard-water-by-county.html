How Hard Is DFW Water, Really? A County-by-County Breakdown

Water Cowboys Team · 6 min read

Every homeowner in Dallas-Fort Worth has heard the phrase "hard water" thrown around — usually by a plumber standing in their kitchen. But almost nobody can tell you what it actually means for their specific ZIP code, because most water treatment companies don't want the conversation to be that simple.

We pulled hardness figures across the ten counties we serve — the same numbers behind the ZIP lookup tool on our homepage — and the spread is bigger than most people expect.

The short version

Every single county in our service area classifies as at least "Hard" on the standard USGS grains-per-gallon (gpg) scale. Nowhere in DFW gets a pass. But how hard varies meaningfully depending on where your water actually comes from.

Denton County runs nearly 40% harder than Tarrant County. That's not a rounding error — it's the difference between water that leaves faint spotting on your glassware and water that scales up a tankless water heater within a couple of years.

Why the difference exists

Hardness isn't random — it's downstream of which utility actually treats your water, and where that utility sources it from. Fort Worth Water draws primarily from a mix of reservoirs with lower mineral content, which is a big part of why Tarrant County consistently reads lowest across our whole service area.

Most of the northern suburbs — Plano, Frisco, McKinney, Allen, Wylie — are served by the North Texas Municipal Water District (NTMWD), which sources differently and consistently reads harder. Denton, further north still, layers its own municipal treatment on top of a similar water source, which is part of why it tops our list.

The practical takeaway: if you live in Frisco, Plano, McKinney, or Denton, you're not imagining it — your water genuinely is harder than a friend's house in Fort Worth or Arlington. It's not a sales pitch, it's just geography and infrastructure.

What "hard" actually costs you

The USGS scale runs from Soft (under 1 gpg) to Very Hard (10.5+ gpg). Once you're above roughly 7 gpg, most plumbing professionals consider a softener worth the investment — not because the water is unsafe, but because of what mineral buildup does to infrastructure over years: reduced water heater lifespan, clogged aerators and showerheads, and soap that doesn't fully rinse off skin and hair. We go deeper on the real mechanism and the actual numbers behind each of these if you want the full picture.

At DFW's regional average of roughly 12-13 gpg, that's not a marginal call. It's solidly in the range where the buildup is measurable within a few years, not a decade.
